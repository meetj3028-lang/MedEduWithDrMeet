Creatinine and eGFR Explained: Understanding Kidney Function

Learn what creatinine measures, how eGFR is estimated and why urine albumin is also important when assessing kidney health.

    1. What do the kidneys do?

    The kidneys filter blood, remove waste products and excess fluid through urine, help regulate electrolytes and acid–base balance, contribute to blood pressure regulation, and produce hormones involved in red blood cell production and bone health.

    2. What is creatinine?

    Creatinine is a waste product formed mainly from normal muscle metabolism. It enters the bloodstream at a relatively steady rate and is removed primarily by the kidneys. A blood creatinine test is commonly used as part of kidney function assessment.

    Remember: Creatinine is a useful marker, but it is not a direct measurement of kidney function. Its level is influenced by muscle mass, diet, age, medicines and other factors.

    3. Why can blood creatinine increase?

    • Reduced kidney filtration, including acute kidney injury or chronic kidney disease.
    • Dehydration or reduced blood flow to the kidneys.
    • Urinary tract obstruction, depending on its severity and duration.
    • High muscle mass, recent strenuous exercise or muscle injury.
    • Recent intake of cooked meat and certain medicines that affect creatinine handling.

    A raised creatinine should be interpreted with previous results, urine output, symptoms, medicines and the clinical situation. A rapid rise may be more concerning than a stable value.

    4. What is eGFR?

    eGFR means estimated glomerular filtration rate. It is calculated using blood creatinine and factors such as age and sex, with equations designed to estimate how well the kidneys filter blood. Many laboratories use the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine equation or another validated equation.

    eGFR is usually reported in mL/min/1.73 m². It is an estimate rather than a direct measurement. When creatinine is changing rapidly, such as during acute kidney injury, eGFR calculations may be unreliable because they assume a relatively stable creatinine level.

    5. How are eGFR values classified?

    eGFR categoryValue (mL/min/1.73 m²)Description
    G190 or higherNormal or high
    G260–89Mildly decreased
    G3a45–59Mildly to moderately decreased
    G3b30–44Moderately to severely decreased
    G415–29Severely decreased
    G5Below 15Kidney failure range

    Important: One eGFR result below 60 does not automatically establish chronic kidney disease. CKD generally requires abnormalities of kidney structure or function, with implications for health, present for at least 3 months. An eGFR of 60 or higher may still be associated with CKD if markers of kidney damage are present.

    6. Why is urine ACR important?

    Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) checks for small amounts of albumin leaking into the urine. Albumin in urine can be an early sign of kidney damage, including in people with diabetes or high blood pressure. It adds information that blood creatinine and eGFR alone may miss.

    Urine ACR categoryValueMeaning
    A1Below 30 mg/gNormal to mildly increased
    A230–300 mg/gModerately increased
    A3Above 300 mg/gSeverely increased

    ACR may temporarily rise after strenuous exercise, fever, urinary infection, menstruation or acute illness. An abnormal result is often repeated to confirm persistence.

    7. Can creatinine be normal when kidney disease is present?

    Yes. Early kidney disease may be present with a creatinine level within the laboratory reference interval, especially when there is albumin in the urine or another marker of kidney damage. This is why eGFR and urine ACR are important parts of assessment.

    8. What can affect the accuracy of eGFR?

    • Very low or very high muscle mass.
    • Amputation, frailty or severe malnutrition.
    • Pregnancy and rapidly changing kidney function.
    • Some medicines and dietary factors that affect creatinine.

    In selected situations, clinicians may use cystatin C or a combined creatinine–cystatin C equation to improve estimation. Direct measurement of GFR is reserved for selected clinical circumstances.

    9. What can help protect kidney health?

    • Keep blood pressure and blood glucose controlled if they are elevated.
    • Stay appropriately hydrated, following medical advice if fluid restriction is needed.
    • Avoid unnecessary use of medicines that can harm the kidneys, including unsupervised NSAIDs.
    • Do not smoke and maintain regular physical activity.
    • Have kidney tests when recommended, especially if you have diabetes, hypertension or a history of kidney disease.

    Key takeaway

    Creatinine helps estimate kidney filtration, but it has limitations. eGFR gives a more useful estimate of filtration, while urine ACR detects albumin leakage and possible kidney damage. Kidney health is assessed using trends and both blood and urine tests—not a single creatinine number.

    Medical education disclaimer: This article is for general education only and is not a diagnosis or personal treatment plan. CKD classification requires clinical assessment and evidence of chronicity. Discuss abnormal results with a qualified healthcare professional.
