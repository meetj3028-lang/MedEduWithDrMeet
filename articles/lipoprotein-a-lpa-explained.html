Lipoprotein(a) — Lp(a): The Inherited Risk Factor for Heart Disease

Learn what Lp(a) is, why it is largely genetic, who may benefit from testing and how to interpret the result.

    1. What is Lipoprotein(a)?

    Lipoprotein(a), written as Lp(a), is a cholesterol-carrying particle in the blood. It is similar to an LDL particle but has an additional protein called apolipoprotein(a) attached to apolipoprotein B-100.

    Higher Lp(a) levels are associated with increased risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, including heart attack and stroke, and with calcific aortic valve stenosis.

    Remember: Lp(a) is different from ordinary LDL cholesterol. A person can have an acceptable LDL-C result and still have a high Lp(a) level.

    2. Why is Lp(a) called a genetic risk factor?

    Blood Lp(a) concentration is largely determined by inherited variation in the LPA gene. Levels are usually relatively stable throughout adult life and are much less influenced by ordinary lifestyle changes than LDL cholesterol or triglycerides.

    This means a person may have elevated Lp(a) even if they eat well, exercise regularly and have no obvious traditional cardiovascular risk factors. Lifestyle remains important for overall heart health, but it generally does not lower Lp(a) substantially.

    3. What health risks are associated with high Lp(a)?

    Higher Lp(a) is a risk-enhancing factor associated with:

    • Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, including coronary artery disease and heart attack.
    • Ischaemic stroke in some populations and clinical contexts.
    • Calcific aortic valve stenosis.

    Risk is influenced by the Lp(a) level and the person's other risk factors, such as LDL cholesterol, blood pressure, diabetes, smoking and family history.

    4. Who should consider an Lp(a) test?

    Many contemporary guidelines and expert groups support measuring Lp(a) at least once in adulthood, while recommendations vary by region and clinical context. Testing may be particularly useful when there is:

    • Premature cardiovascular disease in the person or close family members.
    • A family history of very high Lp(a) or early heart attack or stroke.
    • Familial hypercholesterolaemia or suspected inherited lipid disorder.
    • Cardiovascular disease that seems disproportionate to conventional risk factors.

    A clinician can help decide whether testing is appropriate and how the result could change prevention decisions. If a high level is found, testing close relatives may be considered.

    5. Does the Lp(a) test require fasting?

    Usually, no. Lp(a) can generally be measured in a non-fasting blood sample. Follow the laboratory's instructions, particularly if other tests are being collected at the same time.

    6. How are Lp(a) results reported?

    Laboratories commonly report Lp(a) in either mg/dL or nmol/L. These units measure different properties: mg/dL is a mass concentration, while nmol/L is a particle concentration.

    Result (commonly used risk threshold)General interpretation
    Below 30 mg/dL or below 75 nmol/LGenerally considered a lower-risk range
    30–50 mg/dL or 75–125 nmol/LIntermediate range in some consensus frameworks
    At least 50 mg/dL or at least 125 nmol/LCommonly considered a risk-enhancing level
    Important about units: Do not convert mg/dL to nmol/L using one fixed multiplier. The size and composition of Lp(a) particles vary between people, so the relationship is not constant. Use the unit and reference range provided by the testing laboratory.

    7. Can diet and exercise reduce Lp(a)?

    Diet, exercise and weight management are essential for cardiovascular health, but they usually have little effect on the inherited Lp(a) level. They can still reduce overall risk by improving blood pressure, glucose control, fitness and other lipid measures.

    Do not use supplements or extreme diets to try to lower Lp(a) without discussing them with a healthcare professional.

    8. What happens if Lp(a) is high?

    Management focuses on reducing overall cardiovascular risk. Depending on the person's risk profile, a clinician may recommend:

    • Careful control of LDL cholesterol, often with evidence-based lipid-lowering treatment when indicated.
    • Management of blood pressure, diabetes and other risk factors.
    • Stopping tobacco use and maintaining regular physical activity and a heart-healthy eating pattern.
    • Assessment of family history and consideration of testing first-degree relatives.

    Some medicines can affect Lp(a) to varying degrees, but treatment choices should be based on proven cardiovascular benefit and the person's overall clinical situation. Dedicated Lp(a)-lowering therapies are an active area of research; availability and approved indications may change.

    9. Is high Lp(a) the same as high LDL?

    No. LDL-C estimates the cholesterol carried within LDL particles. Lp(a) is a separate lipoprotein particle with an added apolipoprotein(a) component. Both can contribute to cardiovascular risk, and both may need to be considered in a comprehensive assessment.

    Key takeaway

    Lp(a) is a largely inherited cholesterol-carrying particle that can increase cardiovascular risk independently of many traditional risk factors. A once-in-adulthood measurement may identify people who could benefit from more intensive management of modifiable risks. Interpret the result using the laboratory's units and a clinician's overall risk assessment.

    Medical education disclaimer: This article is for general education and awareness only. It is not a diagnosis or personal treatment plan. Lp(a) thresholds and testing recommendations may vary by guideline and assay. Discuss your result and family history with a qualified healthcare professional.
