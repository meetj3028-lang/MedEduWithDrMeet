Complete LFT Explained: Bilirubin, ALP, GGT, Albumin and PT/INR

A practical guide to the main tests in a liver panel, what each one tells us and how clinicians interpret patterns together.

    1. What is an LFT?

    LFT stands for liver function test. It usually refers to a group of blood tests used to assess possible liver injury, bile flow problems and aspects of the liver's ability to make important proteins. The exact tests included vary between laboratories.

    Important distinction: ALT, AST, ALP and GGT are mainly markers of injury or cholestasis. Albumin and PT/INR can provide information about synthetic function, although they are affected by other conditions too.

    2. Main components of an LFT

    TestWhat it can indicate
    ALT (SGPT)Relatively liver-specific enzyme; may rise with hepatocellular injury
    AST (SGOT)Enzyme found in liver, muscle and other tissues
    ALPMay rise with bile-duct disease or cholestasis; also comes from bone
    GGTCan support a hepatobiliary source when ALP is elevated; not specific alone
    Total/direct bilirubinReflects bilirubin processing and excretion
    AlbuminMajor protein made by the liver; may be low in chronic liver disease and other conditions
    PT/INRAssesses clotting time; may reflect clotting-factor synthesis but is affected by anticoagulants and vitamin K

    3. What is bilirubin?

    Bilirubin is a yellow pigment formed mainly when old red blood cells are broken down. It travels to the liver, where it is taken up, conjugated (made water-soluble) and excreted into bile.

    • Unconjugated (indirect): Has not yet been conjugated by the liver.
    • Conjugated (direct): Has been processed by the liver and is ready for excretion into bile.

    Raised bilirubin can occur due to increased red-cell breakdown, impaired liver uptake or processing, liver-cell injury, or obstruction to bile flow.

    4. What are ALP and GGT?

    Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) is found in bile ducts and other tissues, especially bone. An elevated ALP may be due to a liver/bile-duct problem or a bone-related cause. Gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) is present in the liver and other tissues. When ALP and GGT are both elevated, a hepatobiliary source becomes more likely. GGT can also rise with alcohol use, medicines and other conditions.

    5. What do albumin and PT/INR tell us?

    Albumin is made by the liver and has a relatively long half-life. Low albumin may occur in chronic liver disease, but also in inflammation, malnutrition, kidney protein loss and other illnesses.

    PT/INR reflects the activity of several clotting factors, many made by the liver. A prolonged PT/INR can occur in significant liver dysfunction, but also with warfarin, vitamin K deficiency and other conditions. INR in a person taking warfarin is interpreted differently from INR in someone not taking anticoagulants.

    6. Recognising common LFT patterns

    PatternTypical picturePossible causes
    HepatocellularALT/AST rise more prominently than ALPViral hepatitis, MASLD, medicine-related injury, autoimmune hepatitis
    CholestaticALP rises more prominently, often with GGTBile-duct obstruction, cholestatic liver diseases, some medicines
    MixedAminotransferases and ALP both elevatedSeveral liver and bile-duct conditions

    Clinicians may use the pattern and degree of enzyme elevation, sometimes with the R ratio, to guide evaluation. No pattern alone establishes the cause.

    7. Does an abnormal LFT always mean liver disease?

    No. AST can rise from muscle injury, ALP can come from bone, and albumin or INR can be abnormal for reasons unrelated to the liver. Some people with chronic liver disease may have near-normal enzyme levels. The full history, examination and additional investigations are important.

    8. What may be checked next?

    Depending on the pattern and clinical situation, a clinician may review alcohol intake, medicines and supplements, metabolic risk factors and symptoms. Further tests may include viral hepatitis screening, ultrasound, autoimmune markers, iron studies or other targeted investigations. Not every person needs every test.

    9. When is medical assessment urgent?

    Seek prompt medical attention for new jaundice, dark urine with pale stools, worsening abdominal pain, persistent vomiting or fever. Confusion, unusual drowsiness, vomiting blood, black stools or significant bleeding require emergency assessment.

    Key takeaway

    An LFT is a panel, not a single test. ALT and AST help identify possible cell injury; ALP and GGT help assess cholestasis; bilirubin reflects processing and excretion; albumin and PT/INR provide information about synthetic function but have other influences. The pattern, trend and patient's clinical picture matter more than any isolated number.

    Medical education disclaimer: This article is for general education and awareness only. It is not a diagnosis or personal treatment plan. Reference intervals vary by laboratory. Discuss abnormal results with a qualified healthcare professional and do not change prescribed medicines without medical advice.
