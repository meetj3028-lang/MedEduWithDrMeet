Iron Studies Explained: Ferritin, Serum Iron, TIBC and Transferrin Saturation

Understand how iron is transported and stored, how iron studies help investigate anaemia, and why ferritin must be interpreted with clinical context.

    1. What are iron studies?

    Iron studies are a group of blood tests used to assess iron availability and iron stores. They commonly include serum ferritin, serum iron, total iron-binding capacity (TIBC) or transferrin, and transferrin saturation (TSAT). They help investigate suspected iron deficiency, iron overload and some patterns of anaemia.

    Remember: No single iron test tells the whole story. Results should be interpreted with the CBC, symptoms, inflammation status, medical history and laboratory reference ranges.

    2. Why does the body need iron?

    Iron is essential for haemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. It is also involved in myoglobin and several enzymes. The body conserves and recycles iron, while small amounts are lost through normal shedding of cells and bleeding.

    3. What is serum ferritin?

    Ferritin is a protein that stores iron. Serum ferritin is commonly used as a marker of the body's iron stores. A low ferritin strongly supports iron deficiency in many clinical settings.

    However, ferritin is also an acute-phase reactant. It can rise with infection, inflammation, liver disease, malignancy and other conditions. Therefore, a normal or high ferritin does not always exclude iron deficiency, particularly when inflammation is present.

    4. What is serum iron?

    Serum iron measures the amount of iron circulating in the blood, most of which is bound to transferrin. It can vary during the day and may be affected by recent iron intake, illness and other factors. Serum iron should not be used alone to diagnose iron deficiency or iron overload.

    5. What are transferrin and TIBC?

    Transferrin is the main protein that transports iron in the blood. TIBC estimates the blood's capacity to bind iron and is commonly used as an indirect measure of transferrin availability.

    In uncomplicated iron deficiency, transferrin and TIBC often increase as the body tries to carry more iron. In inflammation, chronic disease or some liver conditions, TIBC may be normal or low.

    6. What is transferrin saturation (TSAT)?

    TSAT estimates the proportion of transferrin's iron-binding capacity that is occupied by iron. It is calculated using serum iron and TIBC, with the appropriate units:

    TSAT (%) = (Serum iron ÷ TIBC) × 100

    A low TSAT suggests limited circulating iron availability. A high TSAT may prompt assessment for iron overload, but interpretation depends on ferritin, clinical history and the laboratory method.

    These are common patterns, not absolute rules. Iron deficiency and inflammation can occur together, producing mixed results.

    8. Iron deficiency vs anaemia of inflammation

    In absolute iron deficiency, the body's iron stores are depleted, so ferritin is usually low and TIBC often rises. In anaemia of inflammation, inflammatory signals increase hepcidin, which reduces iron absorption and traps iron in storage sites. Serum iron and TSAT may be low even when ferritin is normal or high.

    Some patients have both inflammation and true iron deficiency. Clinicians may use the full pattern, inflammatory markers, reticulocyte haemoglobin or soluble transferrin receptor in selected cases.

    9. What can cause iron deficiency?

    • Chronic blood loss, including heavy menstrual bleeding or gastrointestinal bleeding.
    • Inadequate dietary iron intake.
    • Increased requirements, such as during pregnancy and periods of rapid growth.
    • Reduced absorption, for example in coeliac disease or after some gastrointestinal surgeries.
    • Repeated blood donation or other ongoing blood loss.

    Identifying the cause is important. Iron deficiency in an adult should not automatically be attributed to diet without considering blood loss and other possible causes.

    10. What can cause high ferritin?

    High ferritin does not automatically mean excess body iron. It can rise with inflammation, infection, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, alcohol-related liver injury, kidney disease and some malignancies. Persistently high ferritin—especially with raised TSAT—may warrant evaluation for iron overload and other causes.

    11. Does the test require fasting?

    Requirements vary by laboratory and by the tests ordered. Serum iron and TSAT can be influenced by recent intake and timing, so some clinicians or laboratories may request a morning or fasting sample. Follow the instructions provided by the testing laboratory.

    12. How is iron deficiency treated?

    Treatment depends on the cause and severity. Oral or intravenous iron may be considered in appropriate patients, alongside investigation and management of the underlying cause. Do not start high-dose iron supplements solely from a CBC result or stop prescribed treatment without clinical advice. Iron overdose can be dangerous, particularly for children.

    Key takeaway

    Ferritin reflects iron stores but can rise with inflammation. Serum iron shows circulating iron, TIBC reflects binding capacity, and TSAT estimates how much transferrin is carrying iron. Reading these tests together with CBC and clinical context helps distinguish iron deficiency, inflammation-related iron restriction and possible iron overload.

    Medical education disclaimer: This article is for general education only and is not a diagnosis or personal treatment plan. Reference ranges and diagnostic thresholds vary by laboratory, age, pregnancy and clinical condition. Discuss abnormal results with a qualified healthcare professional.
