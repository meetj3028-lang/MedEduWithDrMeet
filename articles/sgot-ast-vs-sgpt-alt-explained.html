SGOT (AST) vs SGPT (ALT): Understanding Liver Enzymes

Learn what these enzymes do, why their levels rise and how doctors interpret them as part of a liver assessment.

By Dr Meet Joshi · MedEduWithDrMeet

1. What are SGOT and SGPT?

SGOT and SGPT are older names commonly used on laboratory reports:

  • SGOT stands for serum glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase. Its current name is AST (aspartate aminotransferase).
  • SGPT stands for serum glutamic-pyruvic transaminase. Its current name is ALT (alanine aminotransferase).

AST and ALT are enzymes involved in amino-acid metabolism. They are present inside cells. When cells are injured, these enzymes can leak into the bloodstream, causing their measured levels to rise.

Remember: AST and ALT are markers of cell injury, not direct measurements of how well the liver is functioning.

2. Where are AST and ALT found?

ALT is found mainly in the liver, so it is generally more liver-specific. AST is found in the liver but also in skeletal muscle, heart muscle, red blood cells and other tissues. Therefore, an elevated AST may come from outside the liver.

3. Why do AST and ALT increase?

Common causes include:

  • Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD; previously called NAFLD).
  • Alcohol-associated liver disease.
  • Viral hepatitis, including hepatitis A, B and C.
  • Medicines, herbal products or supplements that can cause liver injury.
  • Autoimmune hepatitis and other inflammatory liver conditions.
  • Muscle injury or strenuous exercise, especially for AST.

The degree of elevation, duration, symptoms and other test results help guide the evaluation. A mild elevation does not automatically mean severe liver disease.

5. What is the AST/ALT ratio?

The AST/ALT ratio is calculated by dividing AST by ALT. It can provide a clue in selected clinical situations, but it cannot establish a diagnosis on its own.

  • ALT may be higher than AST in many causes of hepatocellular injury, including some cases of viral hepatitis and MASLD.
  • AST greater than ALT can occur in alcohol-associated liver disease, advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis, and non-liver causes such as muscle injury.

A ratio above 2 is classically associated with alcohol-associated hepatitis in the appropriate clinical setting, but it is not diagnostic and is not present in every patient. The history, examination and complete laboratory profile are essential.

6. What are normal AST and ALT levels?

Reference intervals vary by laboratory, assay, age and sex. Many adult laboratories use approximate upper limits around 35–40 U/L, but some professional guidance suggests lower healthy ALT limits. Always use the reference interval printed on the actual report and interpret it in clinical context.

Important: A result within the laboratory reference range does not rule out every liver condition. Some people with chronic liver disease can have normal AST and ALT.

7. Do AST and ALT tell us whether the liver is functioning properly?

Not by themselves. They indicate possible cell injury. Other tests provide different information:

  • Bilirubin: Helps assess bilirubin processing and excretion; it may rise in liver disease or bile-duct obstruction, among other causes.
  • Albumin: A protein made by the liver; a low level can occur in chronic liver dysfunction but also in inflammation, kidney disease and malnutrition.
  • PT/INR: Can help assess the liver's ability to produce clotting factors, although medicines and other conditions also affect it.
  • ALP and GGT: Help evaluate patterns that may involve bile ducts or cholestasis.

These tests are often grouped as a liver panel or liver function tests, although not every component directly measures liver function.

8. What happens if AST or ALT is high?

The next step depends on how high the levels are, whether they are persistent, the patient's symptoms and risk factors. A clinician may review alcohol intake, medicines and supplements, assess metabolic risk, and order tests for viral hepatitis, ultrasound or other investigations when indicated.

Do not stop prescribed medicines or take “liver detox” products based on an isolated result. Some supplements can themselves cause liver injury.

9. When should medical assessment be urgent?

Seek urgent medical assessment for yellowing of the eyes or skin with worsening illness, confusion or unusual drowsiness, severe abdominal pain, repeated vomiting, bleeding, or rapidly worsening symptoms. The urgency depends on the full clinical picture, not just the enzyme number.

Key takeaway

AST (SGOT) and ALT (SGPT) are enzymes that can rise when cells are injured. ALT is more liver-specific, while AST can also come from muscle and other tissues. Their levels and ratio are clues—not diagnoses—and must be interpreted with bilirubin, ALP, GGT, albumin, PT/INR, history and clinical findings.

Medical education disclaimer: This article is for general education and awareness only. It is not a diagnosis or personal treatment plan. Laboratory reference intervals vary. Discuss abnormal results with a qualified healthcare professional and do not change prescribed medicines without medical advice.
