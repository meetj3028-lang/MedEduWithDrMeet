Antibiotic Resistance: Why Are Antibiotics Failing?

Antibiotics have transformed modern medicine. But when bacteria develop ways to survive medicines designed to kill them, infections become harder to treat. Understanding resistance is the first step towards protecting these life-saving drugs.

By Dr Meet Joshi · MedEduWithDrMeet · Educational article

1. What is antibiotic resistance?

Antibiotic resistance happens when bacteria change or acquire defence mechanisms that allow them to survive an antibiotic that would normally kill them or stop their growth. The medicine becomes less effective against those bacteria.

Important: It is the bacteria that become resistant—not the patient's body. A resistant infection can be more difficult to treat and may require different medicines, tests, closer monitoring or specialist care.

2. How does resistance develop?

Resistance can arise naturally through genetic changes. When bacteria are exposed to an antibiotic, susceptible bacteria may be killed or suppressed, while bacteria with resistance traits can survive, multiply and spread. Resistance genes can also move between bacteria.

Antibiotic exposure creates selection pressure. The more antibiotics are used unnecessarily or incorrectly, the more opportunities there are for resistant bacteria to be selected and spread. This does not mean a patient should avoid a medically necessary antibiotic: appropriate antibiotics save lives.

3. Why is antibiotic resistance increasing?

  • Unnecessary use: Taking antibiotics for viral illnesses such as common colds and flu. Antibiotics do not treat viruses.
  • Incorrect use: Using an antibiotic without appropriate medical advice, taking someone else's medicine, using leftover antibiotics, or not following the prescribed dose and schedule.
  • Inappropriate prescribing: Choosing an antibiotic when it is not needed, or selecting an unsuitable drug, dose or duration for the infection.
  • Spread of resistant germs: Poor infection prevention and control, inadequate hand hygiene, and gaps in water, sanitation and hygiene can help resistant organisms spread.
  • Use beyond human healthcare: Antibiotic use in animals and agriculture, and environmental contamination, are also part of the wider One Health challenge.

4. What happens when antibiotics stop working?

Infections caused by resistant bacteria can be harder to treat. They may lead to longer illness, longer hospital stays, more complex treatment, higher costs and an increased risk of complications. Resistance can also make procedures such as surgery, cancer treatment and intensive care riskier because these depend on effective infection prevention and treatment.

5. Do antibiotics work for colds, flu or every fever?

No. Antibiotics act against bacteria, not viruses. Colds and influenza are viral illnesses, so antibiotics do not cure them. A fever alone does not establish whether an illness is bacterial or viral. The cause should be assessed using the history, examination and investigations when clinically indicated.

Remember: Yellow or green nasal mucus by itself does not prove that an infection is bacterial and does not automatically mean antibiotics are needed.

6. How can we help prevent antibiotic resistance?

  • Use antibiotics only when a qualified healthcare professional determines they are needed.
  • Take the medicine exactly as prescribed. If you miss a dose, develop side effects or are unsure how long to take it, contact your prescriber or pharmacist rather than changing the regimen yourself.
  • Never share antibiotics or use leftover medicines from a previous illness.
  • Do not pressure healthcare professionals to prescribe antibiotics when they are not indicated.
  • Keep vaccinations up to date, practise hand hygiene, prepare food safely and follow infection-prevention advice.
  • Healthcare facilities should strengthen infection prevention and control, microbiology and diagnostic capacity, surveillance and antimicrobial stewardship.
  • Use antibiotics responsibly in animal health and agriculture, guided by veterinary and public-health standards.

7. What is antimicrobial stewardship?

Antimicrobial stewardship means coordinated efforts to use antimicrobial medicines appropriately. In healthcare, this includes choosing the right medicine for the right patient, infection, dose and route, and using it for the appropriate duration—supported by clinical assessment, diagnostic results, local resistance data and review of the patient's response.

Key takeaway

Antibiotics are precious resources, not medicines for every fever, cold or sore throat. Using them only when needed, taking them as prescribed, preventing infections and strengthening healthcare systems can help preserve their effectiveness for the future.

Medical education disclaimer: This article is for general educational and public-awareness purposes. It is not a diagnosis or an individual treatment plan. Do not start, stop or change an antibiotic without advice from your treating healthcare professional.
