Procalcitonin (PCT) Test Explained: What Does It Tell Us?

Learn how procalcitonin can support assessment of bacterial infection and sepsis—and why it must never replace clinical judgement.

By Dr Meet Joshi · MedEduWithDrMeet

1. What is procalcitonin?

Procalcitonin (PCT) is a precursor of the hormone calcitonin. In healthy people, its blood concentration is usually very low. During many significant bacterial infections, especially systemic infections, procalcitonin can rise because several tissues begin producing it.

Remember: Procalcitonin is a biomarker that may support the assessment of bacterial infection. It is not a bacteria detector and cannot diagnose sepsis by itself.

2. Why is the PCT test ordered?

Depending on the clinical situation, clinicians may use PCT as one part of the assessment of a suspected systemic bacterial infection, or to help guide antibiotic decisions in selected settings. It may also be measured serially to look at trends during treatment.

3. Does a high PCT mean bacterial infection?

A raised PCT can support the possibility of bacterial infection, particularly when the patient has signs of systemic illness. However, it is not perfectly specific. PCT may also rise in some non-infectious conditions, including major trauma, surgery, burns, shock and certain inflammatory states.

Some localised bacterial infections may produce only a small rise. Early in an infection, PCT may still be low because the level takes time to increase.

4. What do PCT values mean?

Laboratories and clinical protocols may use different thresholds depending on the assay and the clinical setting. The following are examples of commonly encountered interpretive bands—not universal diagnostic cut-offs:

PCT resultGeneral context (must be interpreted clinically)
Below 0.1 ng/mLLow concentration; does not exclude infection
0.1–0.25 ng/mLRelatively low concentration in some respiratory-infection protocols
0.25–0.5 ng/mLMay increase suspicion in some protocols
Above 0.5 ng/mLMay support concern for systemic bacterial infection in the right context

These example bands are not a stand-alone sepsis scale. Different guidelines use different decision thresholds, and clinical decisions should follow validated local protocols.

Safety point: A low PCT must not be used to withhold urgent antibiotics from a patient with suspected sepsis or severe infection when clinical assessment indicates treatment is needed.

6. Can PCT diagnose sepsis?

No. Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated response to infection. Diagnosis is based on clinical assessment and evidence of organ dysfunction, not on a single biomarker. PCT may contribute supporting information, but it cannot confirm or rule out sepsis alone.

7. Can PCT guide antibiotic treatment?

In selected clinical settings, validated PCT-guided protocols may help clinicians decide when to reassess or discontinue antibiotics, particularly when used with clinical improvement and other evidence. It should not be used as the sole reason to start antibiotics, delay treatment or stop treatment.

Antibiotic decisions depend on the suspected source, severity, microbiology, imaging where indicated, response to treatment and local antimicrobial guidance.

8. What affects PCT levels?

  • Timing of the test in relation to the onset of illness.
  • Severity and site of infection.
  • Major surgery, trauma, burns or shock.
  • Some non-infectious inflammatory conditions.
  • Kidney dysfunction and other patient-specific factors.

Serial results may sometimes be more informative than a single measurement, but trends still need clinical interpretation.

Key takeaway

Procalcitonin can be a useful supporting biomarker in selected cases of suspected bacterial infection and antibiotic stewardship. It does not diagnose sepsis, reliably exclude every bacterial infection or replace clinical judgement. Never delay urgent assessment or treatment because of a low PCT result.

Medical education disclaimer: This article is for general education and awareness only. It is not a diagnosis or treatment protocol. In a person with suspected sepsis or severe infection, urgent clinical assessment and management take priority over biomarker results.
