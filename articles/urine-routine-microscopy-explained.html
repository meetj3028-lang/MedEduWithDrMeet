Urine Routine and Microscopy Explained: How to Read a Urine Report

A simple guide to physical, chemical and microscopic urine examination—and what abnormal findings may mean.

By Dr Meet Joshi · MedEduWithDrMeet

1. What is a urine routine and microscopy test?

Urine routine examination (often called urine R/M or urinalysis) is a group of tests that examines a urine sample. It usually includes physical appearance, chemical dipstick testing and microscopic examination of the sediment. It can help identify clues to urinary tract infection, kidney disease, diabetes, stones and other conditions.

Remember: A urine report is a set of clues, not a diagnosis. Symptoms, sample quality, examination and sometimes urine culture or blood tests are needed to interpret abnormal findings.

2. Physical examination

ParameterWhat it may indicate
ColourUsually pale yellow to amber; foods, medicines, blood, bilirubin and hydration can change colour
AppearanceUsually clear; cloudiness may occur with cells, crystals, bacteria or contamination
OdourCan vary with diet, medicines and concentration; odour alone does not diagnose infection

3. Specific gravity (SG)

Specific gravity estimates how concentrated the urine is. It is influenced by fluid intake, kidney concentrating ability and substances dissolved in the urine. A commonly used reference interval is approximately 1.005–1.030, but ranges vary by laboratory and method.

Low values may occur with high fluid intake or impaired concentrating ability. High values may occur with dehydration or increased dissolved substances such as glucose or protein. Interpret SG with the clinical context and other urine findings.

4. Urine pH

Urine pH reflects how acidic or alkaline the urine is. A commonly reported range is around pH 4.5–8.0. Diet, medicines, timing, specimen handling and some urinary infections can influence it. Urine pH alone cannot diagnose a UTI or identify a particular stone type.

5. Chemical examination (dipstick)

ParameterWhat a positive or abnormal result may mean
ProteinMay indicate albumin or other proteins; can occur transiently with fever, exercise or illness, or with kidney disease
GlucoseMay occur when blood glucose is high or with renal glucose-handling conditions; assess with blood glucose/HbA1c
KetonesMay occur with fasting, vomiting, low-carbohydrate intake or insulin deficiency; interpret with glucose and symptoms
Blood / haemoglobinMay reflect red blood cells, free haemoglobin or myoglobin; microscopy helps clarify the finding
Leukocyte esteraseSuggests white blood cells; can support suspicion of urinary inflammation or infection
NitriteMay be positive with certain nitrate-reducing bacteria; a negative result does not exclude UTI
BilirubinMay indicate conjugated bilirubin in urine and warrants assessment in the appropriate context
UrobilinogenMay vary in liver and haemolytic conditions; interpret with other tests

6. Microscopic examination

Microscopy examines cells, casts, crystals and microorganisms in the urine sediment. The reported reference ranges depend on the laboratory, magnification and method.

FindingPossible significance
Pus cells (WBCs)May occur with UTI or other urinary tract inflammation; contamination is also possible
RBCsMay occur with stones, infection, kidney disease, trauma, menstruation contamination or other causes of haematuria
Epithelial cellsSmall numbers may be expected; numerous squamous cells can suggest sample contamination
BacteriaMay represent infection or contamination; symptoms and culture help determine significance
Hyaline castsSmall numbers can occur in healthy people, including after exercise or dehydration
RBC castsCan indicate bleeding originating in the kidney, often requiring prompt evaluation for glomerular disease
WBC castsMay be seen with kidney inflammation, including pyelonephritis or interstitial nephritis
CrystalsMay occur in healthy urine or with stone risk; significance depends on crystal type, quantity, pH and clinical context

7. Does pus cells plus bacteria always mean UTI?

No. A UTI diagnosis depends on symptoms, risk factors and sometimes urine culture. Bacteria or white blood cells may be present because of contamination or asymptomatic bacteriuria. In most non-pregnant adults, asymptomatic bacteriuria is not routinely treated, although specific groups and clinical situations require different management. Follow local clinical guidance.

8. How should a urine sample be collected?

  • Use a clean, sterile container supplied by the laboratory.
  • Follow the laboratory's instructions for cleaning and collection.
  • For a midstream clean-catch sample, begin urinating, then collect the middle portion without touching the inside of the container.
  • Close the lid securely and deliver the sample promptly; if there is a delay, follow the laboratory's storage instructions.
  • Tell the clinician or laboratory if menstruation or another factor may have contaminated the sample.

9. When may a urine culture be needed?

Urine culture may be appropriate when symptoms suggest UTI, infection is recurrent or complicated, the patient is pregnant, or the clinician needs to identify the organism and antibiotic susceptibility. A routine urine report cannot reliably identify the causative organism or determine antibiotic sensitivity.

10. When should medical review be prompt?

Seek medical assessment for visible blood in urine, fever with flank or back pain, painful urination with worsening illness, reduced urine output, or urinary symptoms during pregnancy. Severe illness, confusion, fainting or inability to keep fluids down requires urgent care.

Key takeaway

Urine R/M combines physical, chemical and microscopic findings. Protein, glucose, blood, leukocytes, nitrite, cells and casts each provide different clues. No single abnormal result should be interpreted in isolation; sample quality, symptoms and further tests such as urine culture or urine ACR may be important.

Medical education disclaimer: This article is for general education only and is not a diagnosis or personal treatment plan. Reference ranges vary between laboratories. Do not start antibiotics or other medicines based only on a urine report; consult a qualified healthcare professional.
