Potassium Blood Test Explained: Normal Range, High and Low Potassium

Potassium is essential for nerve signals, muscle contraction and the electrical activity of the heart. Even a relatively small change in blood potassium can matter—especially when the change is rapid or severe.

By Dr Meet Joshi · Medical education · Last reviewed: 27 November 2026

On this page

    1. What is potassium?

    Potassium (K⁺) is the major positively charged ion inside cells. It helps maintain the electrical gradient across cell membranes, allowing nerves to transmit signals and muscles—including the heart—to work normally. The kidneys play a major role in maintaining potassium balance by adjusting how much is excreted in urine.

    Why it matters: Both low potassium (hypokalaemia) and high potassium (hyperkalaemia) can disturb cardiac electrical activity and may cause dangerous arrhythmias.

    2. What does the potassium blood test measure?

    The test measures the concentration of potassium in a blood sample. It is commonly included in an electrolyte panel or basic/comprehensive metabolic panel. It may be ordered to assess or monitor kidney disease, heart conditions, blood pressure disorders, diabetes, fluid losses, or medicines that can affect potassium.

    3. What is the normal potassium range?

    A commonly used adult reference interval is approximately 3.5–5.0 mmol/L, but laboratories may use slightly different limits. MedlinePlus lists a typical range of 3.7–5.2 mEq/L. Always interpret the result using the reference interval printed by the laboratory and the patient's clinical condition.

    Result (illustrative)General termMeaning
    Below the laboratory lower limit (often <3.5 mmol/L)HypokalaemiaPotassium below the reference range

    For potassium, mmol/L and mEq/L have the same numerical value because potassium carries a single positive charge.

    4. What causes low potassium (hypokalaemia)?

    Low potassium may result from potassium loss, movement of potassium from blood into cells, or less commonly inadequate intake in combination with other factors. Common causes include:

    • Vomiting or prolonged diarrhoea
    • Diuretic medicines, especially loop and thiazide diuretics
    • Excessive laxative use
    • Increased urinary potassium loss in some kidney or hormonal disorders
    • Low magnesium, which can make hypokalaemia difficult to correct
    • Shift of potassium into cells, for example with insulin or beta-2 agonist treatment in certain settings

    5. What symptoms can low potassium cause?

    Mild hypokalaemia may cause no symptoms. As the level falls or the change occurs quickly, possible features include fatigue, muscle weakness, cramps, constipation, tingling and palpitations. Severe hypokalaemia can cause marked weakness or paralysis and potentially life-threatening cardiac rhythm disturbances.

    6. What causes high potassium (hyperkalaemia)?

    High potassium commonly occurs when the kidneys cannot remove enough potassium, when potassium shifts out of cells, or due to a combination of factors. Causes and contributors include:

    • Acute kidney injury or chronic kidney disease
    • Medicines such as ACE inhibitors, ARBs, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (for example spironolactone), and some other drugs
    • Metabolic acidosis or insulin deficiency in selected clinical situations
    • Major tissue injury, burns, rhabdomyolysis or tumour lysis
    • Adrenal insufficiency or reduced aldosterone activity
    • Potassium supplements or potassium-containing salt substitutes, particularly in people with impaired kidney function or interacting medicines

    7. What symptoms can high potassium cause?

    Hyperkalaemia may be silent, even when clinically important. Some people experience muscle weakness, fatigue, nausea, tingling or palpitations. Severe or rapidly developing hyperkalaemia can impair cardiac conduction and cause life-threatening arrhythmias or cardiac arrest.

    Urgent warning: A markedly abnormal potassium result, new palpitations, fainting, chest discomfort, severe weakness or an ECG abnormality needs urgent medical assessment. Do not wait for symptoms if a laboratory or clinician has flagged a potentially dangerous potassium level.

    8. How does potassium affect the ECG?

    Potassium abnormalities alter myocardial cell membrane potentials and cardiac conduction. In hyperkalaemia, possible ECG findings include tall, narrow, peaked T waves, PR prolongation, flattening or disappearance of P waves, QRS widening and, in extreme cases, a sine-wave pattern or malignant arrhythmias. In hypokalaemia, possible findings include ST depression, T-wave flattening or inversion, prominent U waves and apparent QT/QU prolongation.

    Clinical pearl: ECG changes are not perfectly sensitive and do not reliably predict the potassium concentration. A normal ECG does not exclude dangerous hyperkalaemia and should not be used alone to reassure or delay appropriate assessment.

    9. Can a potassium result be falsely high?

    Yes. Pseudohyperkalaemia means the measured potassium is falsely elevated in the sample even though the circulating potassium may not be high. It can occur with haemolysis during blood collection or handling, prolonged tourniquet use, repeated fist clenching, or very high platelet or white blood cell counts.

    If the result does not fit the clinical picture, the treating team may repeat the sample using careful collection and prompt processing. However, when severe hyperkalaemia is suspected, confirmation must not create a dangerous delay in urgent care.

    10. What other tests may be considered?

    The next tests depend on the patient's symptoms, medicines, medical history and potassium result. A clinician may consider:

    • ECG and cardiac monitoring when hyperkalaemia is significant or symptoms/risk factors are present
    • Creatinine, eGFR and urea to assess kidney function
    • Other electrolytes, particularly sodium, bicarbonate and magnesium
    • Blood glucose and acid–base assessment when clinically indicated
    • Medication and supplement review, including potassium-containing salt substitutes

    11. Key takeaways

    • Potassium is essential for nerve, muscle and cardiac electrical function.
    • Use the laboratory's own reference interval; a typical adult range is around 3.5–5.0 mmol/L, but it varies.
    • Vomiting, diarrhoea and diuretics can contribute to low potassium; kidney dysfunction and certain medicines can contribute to high potassium.
    • Both severe hypokalaemia and hyperkalaemia can cause dangerous arrhythmias.
    • Consider pseudohyperkalaemia when a result is unexpected, but do not delay emergency assessment when severe hyperkalaemia is suspected.
    • Never start potassium supplements, stop prescribed medicines or make major dietary changes solely from an online interpretation.
