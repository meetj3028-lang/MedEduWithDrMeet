CBC Explained: Complete Blood Count — Hb, RBC, WBC and Platelets

A practical guide to the main components of a complete blood count and what abnormal results may indicate.

    1. What is a CBC?

    A complete blood count (CBC), also called a full blood count (FBC), is a common blood test that measures the number and characteristics of different blood cells. It generally includes red blood cells, white blood cells, haemoglobin, haematocrit, red-cell indices and platelets. Some laboratories also report a white-cell differential count.

    Remember: A CBC can provide clues about anaemia, infection, inflammation, blood loss and bone-marrow disorders. It rarely establishes the cause by itself.

    2. Haemoglobin (Hb)

    Haemoglobin is the iron-containing protein inside red blood cells that carries oxygen. A low Hb is called anaemia. A high Hb may occur with dehydration, chronic low oxygen levels, smoking, living at high altitude or certain blood disorders.

    Commonly used adult thresholds for anaemia are below 13 g/dL in men and below 12 g/dL in non-pregnant women, but interpretation depends on age, pregnancy, altitude, laboratory and clinical context. Pregnancy has trimester-specific considerations.

    3. Red blood cell count (RBC)

    The RBC count measures the number of red blood cells in a given volume of blood. It is interpreted together with Hb, haematocrit and red-cell indices. A count alone does not diagnose anaemia: for example, some people with thalassaemia trait have a relatively high RBC count despite a low MCV.

    4. Haematocrit (HCT / PCV)

    Haematocrit, also called packed cell volume (PCV), is the proportion of blood volume occupied by red blood cells. It may be low in anaemia or high in dehydration and some conditions that increase red-cell mass. Hydration status can affect the result.

    5. Red-cell indices: MCV, MCH, MCHC and RDW

    IndexMeaningWhat an abnormal result may suggest
    MCVMean corpuscular volume; average RBC sizeLow: microcytosis, often iron deficiency or thalassaemia trait. High: macrocytosis, e.g. B12/folate deficiency, alcohol, liver or thyroid disease, or medicines
    MCHMean corpuscular haemoglobin; average Hb amount per RBCOften low in microcytic/hypochromic states
    MCHCMean corpuscular haemoglobin concentrationMay be low in hypochromia; unusually high values need review for specific conditions or sample artefact
    RDWRed-cell distribution width; variation in RBC sizeHigh RDW means greater size variation; can occur in nutritional deficiency or mixed causes

    These indices help classify anaemia, but further tests—such as ferritin, iron studies, vitamin B12, folate, reticulocyte count or a blood film—may be needed to identify the cause.

    6. White blood cell count (WBC / TLC)

    White blood cells help defend the body against infection and participate in immune and inflammatory responses. The total leukocyte count (TLC) measures the overall number of white cells. A high count is called leukocytosis; a low count is leukopenia.

    Changes can occur with infections, inflammation, stress, smoking, medicines, immune conditions and bone-marrow disorders. The differential count helps identify which white-cell type is responsible.

    7. Differential leukocyte count (DLC)

    Cell typeMain roleGeneral interpretation clues
    NeutrophilsImportant in defence against many bacterial and fungal infectionsMay rise with bacterial infection, inflammation, stress or steroids; low counts can increase infection risk
    LymphocytesInclude B cells, T cells and natural killer cellsMay rise in some viral infections and other conditions; low levels have several possible causes
    MonocytesParticipate in immune response and removal of damaged cellsMay rise during recovery from infection or in chronic inflammatory conditions
    EosinophilsParticipate in allergic and parasitic responsesMay rise with allergy, asthma, drug reactions and some parasitic infections
    BasophilsRelease mediators involved in allergic and inflammatory responsesAbnormal increases are uncommon and need context-specific assessment

    Absolute counts are often more clinically useful than percentages alone. A percentage can look high simply because another cell type is reduced.

    8. Platelet count (PLT)

    Platelets help form the initial plug that stops bleeding. A low platelet count is called thrombocytopenia; a high count is thrombocytosis. Causes include infections, medicines, immune conditions, nutritional deficiencies, blood loss, inflammation and bone-marrow disorders.

    The risk of bleeding or clotting cannot be judged from the platelet count alone. The degree of change, trend, symptoms, platelet function and underlying cause matter.

    9. What is a peripheral blood smear?

    A peripheral blood smear is a microscopic examination of blood cells on a slide. It can show cell size, shape, colour, immature cells, abnormal white cells and platelet appearance. It may be requested when CBC results are abnormal or when the clinical picture suggests a blood disorder.

    10. How should a CBC be interpreted?

    • Start with Hb and haematocrit to assess possible anaemia or increased red-cell concentration.
    • Use MCV and other red-cell indices to classify the pattern.
    • Review total WBC and the absolute differential counts.
    • Check platelet count and compare with previous results.
    • Correlate with symptoms, examination, medications and other tests.

    For example, low Hb with low MCV may prompt evaluation for iron deficiency or thalassaemia, while high MCV may prompt assessment of B12, folate, alcohol use, liver and thyroid function, and medicines. These are investigation clues, not diagnoses.

    When should medical review be urgent?

    Seek urgent assessment for severe breathlessness, chest pain, fainting, active or persistent bleeding, black stools, new widespread bruising or pinpoint red-purple spots with fever, or a laboratory report showing a critically abnormal value. Follow the laboratory's critical-result instructions.

    Key takeaway

    A CBC is more than haemoglobin and total WBC. RBC indices, differential counts and platelets provide important additional information. Always interpret the complete report with symptoms, previous results and appropriate follow-up tests—not by one isolated number.

    Medical education disclaimer: This article is for general education only and is not a diagnosis or personal treatment plan. Reference ranges vary with age, sex, pregnancy, altitude and laboratory method. Discuss abnormal or critical results with a qualified healthcare professional.
