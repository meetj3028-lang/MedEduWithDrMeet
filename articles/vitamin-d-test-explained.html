Vitamin D Test Explained: 25-OH Vitamin D, Deficiency and Toxicity

The vitamin D test is commonly requested, but two similarly named tests can mean very different things. Here is how to understand the 25-hydroxy vitamin D test and its limitations.

By Dr Meet Joshi · Medical education · Last reviewed: 16 November 2026

On this page

    1. What is vitamin D?

    Vitamin D is a fat-soluble vitamin that helps the body absorb calcium and maintain calcium and phosphate balance. It supports normal bone mineralisation and muscle function. The body can produce vitamin D in the skin after exposure to ultraviolet B (UVB) radiation; it is also obtained from foods and supplements.

    Vitamin D from skin, food or supplements is not yet in its active hormonal form. It is first converted in the liver to 25-hydroxyvitamin D and then mainly in the kidneys to 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol).

    2. Which vitamin D test should be ordered?

    Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D], also called calcidiol, is the usual test used to assess vitamin D status. It reflects vitamin D made in the skin and obtained from food and supplements, and it remains in circulation longer than the active form.

    Important distinction: 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D [1,25(OH)₂D or calcitriol] is the active form, but it is generally not the correct test for routine assessment of vitamin D stores. Its level is tightly regulated and may remain normal or rise even when 25(OH)D is low.

    3. Why might a clinician request the test?

    Testing may be considered when there is a clinical reason to suspect deficiency or altered vitamin D metabolism. Examples include:

    • Suspected rickets in children or osteomalacia in adults
    • Bone pain, muscle weakness or relevant biochemical abnormalities, when clinically indicated
    • Conditions associated with malabsorption, such as selected intestinal disorders or bariatric surgery
    • Selected chronic kidney or liver conditions, or medicines and disorders that affect vitamin D metabolism
    • Assessment or follow-up when the result is expected to change clinical management

    Routine screening of every healthy person is not recommended by all professional groups. The decision to test should be based on clinical context and applicable local guidance.

    4. Understanding the result

    Results are commonly reported in nanograms per millilitre (ng/mL) or nanomoles per litre (nmol/L). The conversion is:

    1 ng/mL = 2.5 nmol/L · 1 nmol/L = 0.4 ng/mL

    The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements summarises National Academies (FNB/NASEM) categories as follows. These are population-level reference categories, not a universal treatment target for every individual.

    25(OH)D levelGeneral interpretation (FNB/NASEM)
    Below 12 ng/mL
    (below 30 nmol/L)    		Associated with vitamin D deficiency and risk of deficiency-related bone disease
    12 to below 20 ng/mL
    (30 to below 50 nmol/L)    		Potentially inadequate for some people
    20 ng/mL or more
    (50 nmol/L or more)    		Generally considered adequate for most people for bone and overall health
    Above 50 ng/mL
    (above 125 nmol/L)    		May be associated with potential adverse effects; interpret with clinical context
    Do not over-interpret a single cut-off. Different organisations use different definitions, and the optimal concentration for every health outcome has not been established. Interpret the result alongside symptoms, risk factors, calcium and other relevant investigations, the assay used and the laboratory's reference interval.

    5. What can cause a low result?

    Possible contributors include limited sun exposure, low dietary intake, reduced absorption, obesity, certain medications, and disorders affecting liver or kidney metabolism. A low result does not by itself identify the cause.

    6. What does a very high result mean?

    Excessive vitamin D from supplements can cause toxicity. The major concern is hypercalcaemia (high blood calcium), which may cause nausea, vomiting, poor appetite, constipation, weakness, confusion, excessive thirst, frequent urination and kidney problems. Very high levels require clinical assessment, including review of supplement use and calcium status.

    Do not start high-dose vitamin D or repeat large “loading” doses based only on an online chart. Dose and follow-up depend on age, pregnancy status, medical conditions, medicines and the actual laboratory result.

    7. Does everyone need a vitamin D test?

    No. Testing should be targeted to people in whom the result is likely to guide care. Testing healthy people without a specific indication can lead to unnecessary repeat tests or treatment. Follow local clinical recommendations and individual risk assessment.

    8. Key takeaways

    • 25(OH)D is the standard blood test used to assess vitamin D status.
    • 1,25(OH)₂D is the active form, but it is not generally used to assess vitamin D stores.
    • Always check the units: ng/mL and nmol/L are not interchangeable without conversion.
    • Interpretation thresholds vary; a laboratory number should not be used in isolation.
    • Excess supplementation can be harmful. Seek clinician guidance for testing and treatment.
