CRP Test Explained: What Does C-Reactive Protein Tell Us?

Understand what CRP measures, why doctors order it and what a high result can—and cannot—tell you.

    1. What is CRP?

    CRP stands for C-reactive protein. It is a protein made mainly by the liver. Its level in the blood can rise when inflammation occurs in the body. Inflammation may happen because of an infection, an injury, an autoimmune condition or many other causes.

    Remember: CRP is a marker of inflammation. It does not identify the exact cause or location of inflammation by itself.

    2. Why is a CRP test done?

    A healthcare professional may request CRP to help assess inflammation, support evaluation of a suspected infection or inflammatory condition, or follow the trend of inflammation over time. It is interpreted together with symptoms, examination and other investigations.

    3. What does a high CRP mean?

    A raised CRP indicates that inflammation may be present, but it is non-specific. Possible causes include:

    • Bacterial or viral infections.
    • Autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis.
    • Tissue injury, burns, trauma or surgery.
    • Some chronic inflammatory conditions and other illnesses.

    CRP cannot reliably distinguish a bacterial infection from a viral infection on its own. A high result does not automatically mean antibiotics are needed.

    4. What is a normal CRP level?

    Reference intervals vary between laboratories, test methods and clinical settings. Many standard CRP assays use an upper reference limit around 5 mg/L, but you should always check the reference range printed on the actual laboratory report.

    The size of the result does not provide a diagnosis by itself. A clinician considers how high it is, whether it is rising or falling, the patient's symptoms and the reason the test was ordered.

    5. CRP vs ESR: what is the difference?

    FeatureCRPESR
    Full formC-reactive proteinErythrocyte sedimentation rate
    What it reflectsAcute-phase protein response to inflammationRate at which red blood cells settle in a tube; an indirect inflammation marker
    Change over timeUsually rises and falls relatively quickly as inflammation changesOften changes more slowly and may remain raised longer
    Factors affecting resultInfection, inflammation, tissue injury and other factorsInflammation plus factors such as age, anaemia, pregnancy and red-cell characteristics

    Neither test is perfect. Sometimes both are requested because they provide complementary information.

    6. CRP and hs-CRP: are they the same?

    Both tests measure C-reactive protein, but they are designed for different clinical uses. Standard CRP is commonly used when evaluating significant inflammation or infection. High-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP) can measure much lower concentrations and may be used in selected cardiovascular risk assessments.

    hs-CRP should not be confused with a test that diagnoses a heart attack. It is not a substitute for an ECG or cardiac troponin when acute heart injury is suspected.

    7. Does CRP tell us whether antibiotics are required?

    No. CRP alone cannot decide whether an infection is bacterial or whether antibiotics are appropriate. The decision depends on the clinical assessment, likely source of infection, severity, relevant tests and established treatment guidance. Unnecessary antibiotic use can cause harm and contribute to antimicrobial resistance.

    8. Can CRP be used to monitor treatment?

    In some conditions, repeated CRP measurements can help clinicians assess whether inflammation is improving or worsening. A falling value may be reassuring in the right clinical context, but it does not prove that an infection has cleared. Persistent or rising CRP also needs interpretation alongside the patient's condition.

    Key takeaway

    CRP is a useful, non-specific marker of inflammation. It can help clinicians assess and monitor certain conditions, but it cannot identify the cause on its own, distinguish bacterial from viral infection reliably or determine the need for antibiotics by itself. Always interpret the result with the clinical picture and the laboratory's reference range.

    Medical education disclaimer: This article is for general education and awareness only. It is not a diagnosis or personal treatment plan. Seek medical advice for symptoms or concerns, and do not start or stop medicines based on a CRP result alone.
