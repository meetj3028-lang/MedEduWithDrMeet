Vitamin B12 Deficiency Explained: Symptoms, CBC Changes and Diagnosis

Understand why vitamin B12 is essential, who is at risk, how deficiency affects blood cells and nerves, and how it is diagnosed.

    1. What is vitamin B12?

    Vitamin B12, also called cobalamin, is a water-soluble vitamin needed for DNA synthesis, normal red blood cell formation and healthy nervous-system function. The body cannot make B12, so it must be obtained from food or supplements.

    Remember: Vitamin B12 deficiency can affect both the blood and the nervous system. Neurological symptoms may occur even when anaemia or macrocytosis is absent.

    2. What does vitamin B12 do?

    • Supports DNA synthesis and normal cell division.
    • Helps normal red blood cell development.
    • Supports myelin maintenance and neurological function.
    • Acts as a cofactor in important metabolic pathways, including conversion of methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA and homocysteine to methionine.

    3. What causes vitamin B12 deficiency?

    • Low dietary intake: B12 is naturally found mainly in animal-derived foods. People following strict vegan diets without fortified foods or supplements are at increased risk. Vegetarians may also have inadequate intake depending on their diet.
    • Pernicious anaemia: Autoimmune damage to gastric parietal cells or intrinsic factor can impair B12 absorption.
    • Gastrointestinal conditions or surgery: Conditions affecting the stomach or terminal ileum, and some bariatric or intestinal surgeries, can reduce absorption.
    • Medicines: Long-term metformin and acid-suppressing medicines such as proton pump inhibitors may contribute in some people.
    • Other causes: Older age, some intestinal disorders and rare inherited conditions.

    4. What are the symptoms?

    Symptoms may develop gradually and can include:

    • Tiredness, weakness and reduced exercise tolerance.
    • Pale skin, palpitations or breathlessness due to anaemia.
    • Sore or smooth tongue (glossitis), mouth ulcers or reduced appetite.
    • Numbness, tingling or “pins and needles” in hands and feet.
    • Balance difficulty, unsteady walking or reduced vibration and position sense.
    • Memory, concentration or mood changes in some people.

    Neurological injury can become long-lasting if deficiency is not treated promptly. Do not wait for severe anaemia to develop before seeking assessment when neurological symptoms are present.

    5. How does B12 deficiency affect the CBC?

    Classically, vitamin B12 deficiency causes megaloblastic anaemia, often with macrocytosis. However, the CBC can vary, and a normal MCV does not exclude deficiency.

    CBC / smear findingPossible pattern
    HaemoglobinMay be low due to anaemia
    MCVOften increased (macrocytosis), but may be normal with coexisting iron deficiency or other factors
    RDWMay be increased due to variation in red-cell size
    WBC and plateletsMay be reduced in more significant deficiency (pancytopenia can occur)
    Peripheral smearMay show macro-ovalocytes and hypersegmented neutrophils
    Reticulocyte countOften low or inappropriately normal before treatment

    6. How is vitamin B12 deficiency diagnosed?

    Assessment usually combines symptoms, examination and blood tests. Tests may include:

    • Serum vitamin B12: The initial test in many settings. Results near the laboratory's borderline range may need further evaluation.
    • Methylmalonic acid (MMA): Often rises in functional B12 deficiency; kidney impairment can also raise MMA.
    • Homocysteine: May rise in B12 deficiency but is less specific and can also rise with folate deficiency and other conditions.
    • CBC and blood film: Assess anaemia and cell morphology.
    • Anti-intrinsic factor antibody: Can support a diagnosis of pernicious anaemia when clinically suspected. A negative result does not completely exclude it.

    Exact B12 cut-offs differ between assays and guidelines. Interpret the result using the laboratory range and clinical context rather than relying on one universal number.

    7. Vitamin B12 deficiency vs folate deficiency

    FeatureVitamin B12 deficiencyFolate deficiency
    Megaloblastic anaemiaCan occurCan occur
    Neurological manifestationsCan occur and may become irreversible if untreatedNot a typical feature
    Methylmalonic acidUsually increasedUsually normal
    HomocysteineMay be increasedMay be increased
    Safety point: Do not treat suspected megaloblastic anaemia with folic acid alone until vitamin B12 deficiency has been assessed. Folate can improve the anaemia while neurological damage from untreated B12 deficiency continues.

    8. Who should consider testing?

    Testing may be appropriate for people with compatible symptoms, unexplained macrocytosis or anaemia, peripheral neuropathy, a strict vegan diet without reliable B12 intake, pernicious anaemia risk, gastrointestinal surgery or malabsorption, or long-term use of medicines associated with low B12 when clinically indicated.

    9. How is it treated?

    Treatment depends on the cause, severity and presence of neurological symptoms. It may involve oral B12 supplements or intramuscular injections. People with significant neurological involvement or absorption problems may need a specific treatment regimen and follow-up. The underlying cause should also be addressed.

    Do not self-prescribe a treatment schedule based only on an online article. A clinician may monitor symptoms, CBC and B12-related markers to assess response.

    10. How can deficiency be prevented?

    • Include reliable B12 sources such as dairy, eggs, meat or fish if these are part of the diet.
    • People avoiding animal products should use B12-fortified foods or a reliable supplement plan.
    • Address malabsorption or autoimmune causes with appropriate medical care.
    • Review long-term medicines and risk factors with a clinician when symptoms or risk warrant testing.

    Key takeaway

    Vitamin B12 is essential for red blood cell production and neurological health. Deficiency can cause macrocytic anaemia, but neurological symptoms may occur even with a normal CBC. Diagnosis may require B12 testing and, in selected cases, MMA or other investigations. Early recognition and appropriate treatment help prevent lasting complications.

    Medical education disclaimer: This article is for general education only and is not a diagnosis or personal treatment plan. Test interpretation and treatment depend on the clinical situation, laboratory method and cause of deficiency. Seek medical assessment for neurological symptoms or significant anaemia.
