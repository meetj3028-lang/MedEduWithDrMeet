D-dimer Test Explained: Blood Clots, DVT and Pulmonary Embolism

What D-dimer measures, when it is useful and why a positive result does not automatically mean a blood clot.

    1. What is D-dimer?

    D-dimer is a small protein fragment produced when the body breaks down cross-linked fibrin. Fibrin forms part of a blood clot. When a clot is formed and then broken down by the body's fibrinolytic system, D-dimer can be released into the bloodstream.

    Remember: D-dimer is a marker of clot formation and breakdown. It does not show exactly where a clot is located.

    2. Why is the D-dimer test ordered?

    D-dimer is commonly used as part of the diagnostic pathway when a clinician is assessing a person for venous thromboembolism (VTE), particularly:

    • Deep vein thrombosis (DVT): A blood clot in a deep vein, usually in the leg.
    • Pulmonary embolism (PE): A clot, usually originating in the venous system, that travels to the pulmonary arteries.

    It is most useful when combined with a clinical pre-test probability assessment. Depending on the risk assessment, imaging or another pathway may be needed instead of—or after—the blood test.

    3. What does a negative D-dimer mean?

    In a person with low or appropriate non-high clinical probability, a negative high-sensitivity D-dimer can help exclude DVT or PE without imaging in validated diagnostic pathways.

    However, a negative result is not suitable for ruling out a clot in every patient. It should not be used alone when clinical probability is high, and the test's sensitivity, assay and clinical pathway matter.

    4. What does a positive D-dimer mean?

    A positive result means D-dimer is above the laboratory's decision threshold. It does not confirm DVT or PE. It indicates that further assessment may be needed, depending on symptoms and clinical probability.

    D-dimer can be elevated in many situations other than VTE, including:

    • Increasing age.
    • Pregnancy and the postpartum period.
    • Recent surgery, trauma or hospitalisation.
    • Infection, inflammation and severe illness.
    • Some cancers and liver disease.

    5. What is the normal value?

    There is no single universal reference limit for every D-dimer assay. Many laboratories report results using a fibrinogen-equivalent unit (FEU) threshold of about 500 ng/mL FEU (equivalent to 0.5 mg/L FEU), but units, assays and cut-offs vary. Always check the unit and reference limit on the actual report.

    Important: FEU and D-dimer units (DDU) are not interchangeable. FEU values are approximately twice DDU values, but use the laboratory's own validated cut-off rather than converting casually.

    6. What is an age-adjusted D-dimer?

    For selected patients aged over 50 years who are being evaluated for suspected PE using an appropriate validated pathway, an age-adjusted threshold may improve specificity while maintaining safety. A commonly used formula is age × 10 ng/mL FEU for patients older than 50 years, when the assay's standard threshold is 500 ng/mL FEU.

    This formula is not universal for every assay or clinical situation. It should only be used within a validated local diagnostic pathway and interpreted by a clinician.

    7. D-dimer and DVT/PE: what happens next?

    • Low or non-high clinical probability: A suitable negative high-sensitivity D-dimer may exclude VTE in a validated pathway.
    • Positive D-dimer: Imaging is usually considered according to the suspected condition and clinical pathway—for example, compression ultrasound for DVT or CT pulmonary angiography for PE.
    • High clinical probability: Clinicians generally proceed to appropriate imaging rather than relying on D-dimer to rule out VTE.

    8. Does D-dimer monitor blood-thinner treatment?

    D-dimer is not a routine test for adjusting anticoagulant doses. Decisions about anticoagulant treatment, duration and monitoring depend on the diagnosis, clinical factors, bleeding risk and the specific medicine. Do not start, stop or change blood thinners based on a D-dimer result alone.

    9. When should someone seek urgent care?

    Sudden breathlessness, chest pain—especially pain that worsens with breathing—coughing up blood, fainting, or new one-sided leg swelling and pain may require urgent medical assessment. Severe breathlessness, collapse or significant chest pain should be treated as an emergency. Do not wait for a D-dimer test if serious symptoms are present.

    Key takeaway

    D-dimer helps clinicians assess possible blood clots, especially DVT and pulmonary embolism. A negative result can help rule out VTE in appropriately selected patients with non-high clinical probability. A positive result is non-specific and does not diagnose a clot. Clinical probability, assay details and appropriate imaging guide the next step.

    Medical education disclaimer: This article is for general education and awareness only. It is not a diagnostic algorithm or personal medical advice. Suspected DVT or pulmonary embolism requires prompt assessment by a qualified healthcare professional. Seek emergency care for severe or sudden symptoms.
