HbA1c Explained: What Does Your Average Blood Sugar Test Mean?

A simple guide to understanding the HbA1c blood test, its ranges and its limitations.

By Dr Meet Joshi · MedEduWithDrMeet

1. What is HbA1c?

HbA1c stands for glycated haemoglobin. Haemoglobin is the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. Glucose in the blood naturally attaches to haemoglobin. The HbA1c test measures the percentage of haemoglobin with glucose attached.

Because red blood cells usually live for about 2–3 months, HbA1c gives an estimate of average blood glucose over that period. It is not a measurement of your blood sugar at this exact moment.

Easy way to remember: A fasting glucose test is a snapshot. HbA1c is a longer-term summary of blood glucose.

2. Why is the test done?

  • To help screen for and diagnose prediabetes and diabetes.
  • To monitor longer-term glucose management in people with diabetes.
  • To help a healthcare professional review trends alongside symptoms and other glucose readings.

3. Do you need to fast?

Usually, no. HbA1c can generally be tested at any time of day and does not require fasting. If other blood tests are being done at the same visit, follow the laboratory's instructions for those tests.

4. What do the numbers mean?

Commonly used HbA1c categories for diagnosing diabetes in non-pregnant individuals are:

HbA1c resultUsual interpretation
Below 5.7%Below the prediabetes range
5.7%–6.4%Prediabetes range
6.5% or higherDiabetes diagnostic threshold

Unless there are clear symptoms with unequivocal high blood glucose, a result in the diabetes range generally needs confirmation with repeat testing or another diagnostic test. A healthcare professional should interpret the result in context.

Important: These are diagnostic categories, not personal treatment targets. Glucose targets for someone already diagnosed with diabetes are individualised.

5. HbA1c versus fasting blood glucose

  • Fasting plasma glucose: Measures blood glucose after at least 8 hours without calories.
  • HbA1c: Estimates average glucose over roughly 2–3 months and usually does not require fasting.

The tests measure different things. Their results may not always match, and a clinician may choose one or both depending on the situation.

6. What can affect HbA1c accuracy?

HbA1c can be misleading when red blood cell lifespan or haemoglobin is affected. Examples include:

  • Some forms of anaemia, recent blood loss or blood transfusion.
  • Conditions that shorten red blood cell survival, such as haemolysis.
  • Some haemoglobin variants or haemoglobin disorders, depending on the laboratory method.
  • Pregnancy and the period after delivery.
  • Some kidney conditions and other disorders affecting red blood cells.

If the HbA1c result does not fit with glucose readings or the clinical picture, a healthcare professional may recommend plasma glucose-based testing or another approach.

7. Does one high result always confirm diabetes?

Not in every situation. An HbA1c of 6.5% or higher reaches the diagnostic threshold. In the absence of clear symptoms or unequivocal hyperglycaemia, confirmation is generally needed. Diagnosis should be made by a qualified healthcare professional using an appropriate laboratory method.

Key takeaway

HbA1c is a useful marker of average blood glucose over the previous few months. It usually does not require fasting and can help identify prediabetes or diabetes. However, conditions affecting red blood cells and haemoglobin can change how the result should be interpreted. Always consider the whole clinical picture—not just one number.

Medical education disclaimer: This article is for general education and awareness only. It is not a diagnosis or personal treatment plan. Discuss your test results with a qualified healthcare professional.
