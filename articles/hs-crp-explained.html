hs-CRP Explained: The Inflammation Marker Linked to Cardiovascular Risk

Learn how high-sensitivity CRP differs from standard CRP, how it may contribute to cardiovascular risk assessment and what its limitations are.

    1. What is hs-CRP?

    hs-CRP stands for high-sensitivity C-reactive protein. It measures the same protein as a standard CRP test, but the assay is designed to detect lower concentrations in the blood with greater precision.

    CRP is produced mainly by the liver in response to inflammation. Standard CRP is commonly used when clinicians are assessing significant inflammation, such as in infection or inflammatory disease. hs-CRP may be used in selected situations to help assess cardiovascular risk.

    Remember: hs-CRP is not a different protein. It is a more sensitive way of measuring C-reactive protein at low levels.

    2. Why is hs-CRP measured?

    hs-CRP can be considered as one additional factor in estimating the risk of future cardiovascular events in selected adults. It reflects low-grade systemic inflammation, which is associated with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.

    It does not directly detect a blocked artery, diagnose a heart attack or replace assessment of blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, smoking, family history and other established risk factors.

    3. Commonly used hs-CRP categories

    For cardiovascular risk discussions, the following traditional categories have often been used when the person is clinically stable:

    hs-CRP resultTraditional relative risk category
    Below 1 mg/LLower relative risk category
    1–3 mg/LAverage relative risk category
    Above 3 mg/LHigher relative risk category

    These are broad population-based categories, not diagnostic cut-offs or a personal prediction. Current clinical guidance may use hs-CRP selectively as a risk-enhancing factor rather than relying on these categories alone.

    Important: A result above 10 mg/L may reflect an acute infection or another significant inflammatory process. It should not simply be interpreted as cardiovascular risk; a clinician may repeat it when the person is well.

    4. What can raise hs-CRP?

    • Recent infection or acute inflammation.
    • Injury, surgery or tissue damage.
    • Chronic inflammatory or autoimmune conditions.
    • Smoking and excess body fat, particularly abdominal adiposity.
    • Other health and lifestyle factors.

    Because many conditions affect hs-CRP, an elevated value is non-specific. It cannot identify the cause of inflammation.

    6. Does hs-CRP diagnose a heart attack?

    No. hs-CRP is not a test for diagnosing an acute heart attack. When a heart attack is suspected, urgent clinical assessment, ECG and cardiac troponin testing are among the key investigations. Do not delay emergency care while arranging an hs-CRP test.

    7. Who may benefit from the test?

    A clinician may consider hs-CRP when cardiovascular risk remains uncertain after standard risk assessment and the result could help guide a prevention discussion. It is not necessary for everyone, and routine testing is not a substitute for managing established risk factors.

    8. How should the result be interpreted?

    • Interpret it when the person is clinically stable, not during an acute infection or injury.
    • Consider repeating an unexpectedly high result after the acute inflammatory condition has resolved, if clinically appropriate.
    • Review it alongside the complete cardiovascular risk profile and the reason the test was requested.
    • Do not start aspirin, statins or other medicines solely because of one hs-CRP value; treatment decisions require an individual clinical assessment.

    Key takeaway

    hs-CRP is a sensitive measurement of low concentrations of C-reactive protein. It may add information to cardiovascular risk assessment in selected people, but it is non-specific and cannot diagnose heart disease or a heart attack. The result must be interpreted alongside established risk factors and clinical context.

    Medical education disclaimer: This article is for general education and awareness only. It is not a diagnosis or personal treatment plan. Discuss cardiovascular risk and test results with a qualified healthcare professional. Seek emergency care for symptoms suggestive of a heart attack.
