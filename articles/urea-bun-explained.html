Urea and BUN Explained: What Do These Kidney Blood Tests Mean?

Understand how urea is formed, the difference between urea and BUN, and why these values must be interpreted with other kidney tests.

    1. What is urea?

    Urea is a waste product formed in the liver when the body breaks down protein. Nitrogen from ammonia is converted into urea through the urea cycle. Urea travels in the blood to the kidneys, which filter it and remove much of it in urine.

    Remember: Urea is produced mainly in the liver and excreted mainly by the kidneys. Its blood level is influenced by both kidney function and factors that affect urea production or fluid balance.

    2. What is BUN?

    BUN stands for blood urea nitrogen. It measures the amount of nitrogen in the blood that is present in urea. A urea test measures the whole urea molecule, while BUN measures only the nitrogen portion.

    They are related but the numerical values are not the same. Always check whether the laboratory report gives urea or BUN and note the units before interpreting it.

    3. Urea vs BUN: what is the difference?

    TestWhat is measuredCommon reporting units
    UreaEntire urea moleculemg/dL or mmol/L
    BUNNitrogen contained in ureamg/dL

    For results expressed in mg/dL, approximate conversions are:

    BUN (mg/dL) ≈ Urea (mg/dL) ÷ 2.14
    Urea (mg/dL) ≈ BUN (mg/dL) × 2.14

    These conversions apply to the usual mass units shown. Do not use them when units differ; use the laboratory's stated units and conversion guidance.

    4. Why can urea or BUN increase?

    • Reduced kidney filtration: Acute kidney injury or chronic kidney disease can reduce urea removal.
    • Dehydration or reduced kidney blood flow: For example, fluid loss from vomiting, diarrhoea or poor intake.
    • Increased protein breakdown: Severe illness, fever, trauma or other catabolic states.
    • High protein intake: May increase urea generation.
    • Gastrointestinal bleeding: Digested blood protein can increase urea production.
    • Some medicines: Certain medicines may affect kidney function or urea levels.

    A high urea or BUN does not by itself prove kidney failure. The cause must be assessed using the clinical history, examination, creatinine, eGFR, urine findings and other relevant tests.

    5. Why can urea or BUN be low?

    Low values are often less concerning on their own, but may occur with low protein intake, overhydration, pregnancy, severe liver dysfunction or reduced urea production. Interpretation depends on the overall clinical picture.

    6. What is the BUN-to-creatinine ratio?

    The BUN-to-creatinine ratio compares BUN with serum creatinine when both are reported in mg/dL. It may provide a clue about factors such as reduced kidney perfusion, but it is not a stand-alone diagnostic test.

    A higher ratio can occur with dehydration, gastrointestinal bleeding, increased protein breakdown or low muscle mass. A lower ratio may occur in some liver conditions, low protein intake or other settings. Units and clinical context matter; the ratio cannot reliably distinguish all causes of kidney injury.

    7. Is urea enough to assess kidney function?

    No. Urea is affected by diet, hydration, liver function and protein breakdown. Kidney assessment usually includes serum creatinine with estimated GFR (eGFR), urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR), urinalysis and the clinical context. Trends over time are often more useful than a single result.

    8. What happens if urea or BUN is high?

    A clinician may assess hydration, blood pressure, urine output, medicines, symptoms and other blood and urine tests. Treatment depends on the underlying cause—not simply on lowering the urea number. Do not force excessive fluids or stop prescribed medicines without medical advice.

    Key takeaway

    Urea and BUN are related tests but measure different parts of the same molecule. Their levels can change due to kidney function, hydration, diet, liver function and protein breakdown. They should be interpreted alongside creatinine, eGFR, urine tests and the patient's clinical condition.

    Medical education disclaimer: This article is for general education only and is not a diagnosis or personal treatment plan. Reference ranges vary by laboratory and units. Discuss abnormal results with a qualified healthcare professional.
